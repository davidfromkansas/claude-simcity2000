# Break the caps, not the bank

Our cities did not stall because they grew too slowly. They hit limits built into the game, and we never built the things that lift those limits. SimCity 2000 holds residential growth at about **15,000 residents** until the city has stadiums, zoos or marinas. It holds commerce back until there are road links or an airport, and industry until there is a **highway or rail link** or a seaport ([PPP ch.16 p.399](https://archive.org/details/SimCity_2000_Power_Politics_and_Planning_Revised)). In seven attempts we built none of these except one road link. Our best city stopped at about 35,700 and then shrank. The second problem is money. Tax income is close to **population × tax rate × 1.29** a year ([Dargahi Ch.4](https://archive.org/details/SimCity_2000_Power_Politics_and_Planning_Revised)). At 7% that is about **$0.09 per person per year**, so even a 100,000-person city earns only about **$9,000-12,000 a year**. Each $10,000 loan costs about $400-600 a year in interest and never expires on its own. Our 7-10 loans cost more each year than the whole city paid in tax. The third problem is density. Light zones top out at about 10 residents per tile, and our homes averaged 17-18 "population" per tile. At that rate 100,000 people needs over 5,500 home tiles. Dense zones on good land reach 30-40 per tile ([somacon 1996](http://www.somacon.com/p606.php)). Reaching 100,000 by 2050 is realistic: our best attempt grew from 0 to 28,770 in eight years, so speed is not the issue. To get there, the city has to be planned around the caps, dense zoning, land value and a hard limit on loans from the very first year.

A note on sources. The strongest sources are the 1993 Maxis manual and two strategy books from the 1990s. The first is Dargahi & Bremer's *Power, Politics, and Planning* (cited as "PPP" or "Dargahi"). Its Chapter 16 was written with Maxis's designers. The second is Sybex's *Strategies & Secrets* ("S&S"). Fan pages from 1995-96 add rules of thumb, and these are marked as unverified where it matters. No one has published the game's actual formulas from its code, so all the numbers below are documented behaviour, not code.

## Dense zones on high land value hold three to four times more people

Residential buildings come in three sizes. **Light residential only ever builds 1x1 homes, at about 10 residents per tile.** Dense residential starts with 1x1 homes, grows into 2x2 apartments and then into 3x3 towers "if all the other conditions are met" ([PPP p.398](https://archive.org/details/SimCity_2000_Power_Politics_and_Planning_Revised)). The only per-building numbers anyone has published come from a 1996 fan page. A 1x1 home holds **10 people per tile**. A 2x2 building holds **20 per tile at low density (80 per building) or 30 per tile at high density (120 per building)**. A 3x3 building holds **40 per tile (360 per building)** ([somacon 1996](http://www.somacon.com/p606.php)). A modern guide agrees that dense residential is "approximately 40 Sims per acre" (one acre is one tile) ([ludo.guide](https://www.ludo.guide/guide/simcity-2000/dense-residential)). No manual or decoded game code confirms these numbers. Our own saves can check them, though, because the save file stores the type of every building ([dfloer file spec](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/dfloer/SC2k-docs/master/sc2%20file%20spec.md)). Dense zoning costs $10 per tile and light zoning $5 ([Italian manual](https://archive.org/download/manualzilla-id-6163847/6163847_djvu.txt)). Arcologies hold 30,000-65,000 people each, but the game only offers them **at 120,000 people**, so they cannot help us reach 100,000 ([Manual p.112](https://archive.org/details/SimCity.2000-Manual)).

| Building | People per tile | People per building | Tiles needed for 100,000 |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1x1 home (light or dense) | 10 | 10 | ~10,000 |
| 2x2 low density | 20 | 80 | ~5,000 |
| 2x2 high density | 30 | 120 | ~3,300 |
| 3x3 tower | 40 | 360 | ~2,500 |

*Source for the per-tile figures: [somacon 1996](http://www.somacon.com/p606.php), unverified by any primary source.*

A dense zone only grows tall if several things line up. The sources rank them roughly in this order. **Land value comes first**: "Land value is the biggest influence on how dense a zone will grow, and whether or not the zone is at an intersection" ([Weburg 1995](https://patcoston.com/co/handzone.aspx)). Next is **positive demand** for that zone type. Then comes **road access**. One fan claim says a 3x3 lot needs road along three tiles of one side and at least one tile of another side, so corner lots work best. It also needs **other zone types within about 10-15 tiles** ([somacon](http://www.somacon.com/p606.php)). **Water** matters too. The manual says a city without water can exist, but "density will be limited" and "the population won't grow very dense" ([Manual p.110](https://archive.org/details/sc2000_manual)). Finally there is **time**: how long a zone sits next to good surroundings affects whether it grows dense. One old trick is to zone a single 3x3 block, ring it with trees, wait for the tower, then zone the next block ([Weburg](https://patcoston.com/co/handzone.aspx)). The designers' book also admits a quirk in how the game is built: it "prevents high land values from being associated with low-density zones, and low land values from being associated with high-density zones" ([PPP p.113 fn](https://archive.org/details/SimCity_2000_Power_Politics_and_Planning_Revised)). In practice, dense zones on cheap land stay small, and light zones never get the benefit of good land.

## Land value comes from trees, water, parks and safety, and ours was near rock bottom

The manual lists what raises land value. It names **trees**, **water** (lakes, rivers and coast), and **parks**: a small park counts about the same as trees and a big park about twice as much. It also names **all cultural buildings**, **police and fire stations** (within a radius of each), the **City Beautification** ordinance, and, only slightly, homeless shelters ([Italian manual](https://archive.org/download/manualzilla-id-6163847/6163847_djvu.txt)). The designers' chapter adds **closeness to the city centre**, which matters more as the city grows, and hills ([PPP p.403-404](https://archive.org/details/SimCity_2000_Power_Politics_and_Planning_Revised)). Things that lower land value are **pollution, crime and nearby industry**. The biggest polluter is traffic, followed by industry, power plants and ports. Homes and shops make no pollution ([PPP p.404](https://archive.org/details/SimCity_2000_Power_Politics_and_Planning_Revised)). Crime "breeds and spreads in areas of low land value and high population density". A police station covers a radius of up to **about 14 tiles** and must be powered ([PPP pp.404-407](https://archive.org/details/SimCity_2000_Power_Politics_and_Planning_Revised)). A forest or lake only helps while it lasts: "as soon as that forest or water is destroyed, the zone looses all benefit from it" ([Weburg](https://patcoston.com/co/handzone.aspx)).

No source gives the exact weights or how fast land value changes. The save stores land value as a 64x64 grid, one cell for every 2x2 tiles, on a 0-255 scale ([dfloer file spec](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/dfloer/SC2k-docs/master/sc2%20file%20spec.md)). On that scale our cities averaged only **2-28**. Attempt 7, the all-dense city, sat at 1.7-2.9. Attempt 2, our best, peaked near 27. That alone explains why our dense zones rarely grew past small buildings. On our map the river is the one natural asset we have, so the best residential land is along its banks. Trees cost $3 per click and small parks $20, so planting greenery inside every residential block is the cheapest density upgrade in the game ([Manual reference](https://archive.org/details/SimCity.2000-Manual)).

## Tax income is fixed at about nine cents per person, so only population pays the bills

The designers' book gives the official formula as **Property Tax = Land Value × Tax Rate × Population × Scaling Factor**. It then offers a simpler rule that a veteran player found and that fits the game well: **tax per year ≈ total population × tax rate × 1.29** ([Dargahi Ch.4](https://archive.org/details/SimCity_2000_Power_Politics_and_Planning_Revised)). Strategies & Secrets gives a per-zone version: 1.29 × the sum of each zone's rate times that zone's population. It also says the game "calculates property tax based on population", not on what buildings are worth. High land value helps mainly because it lets more people live on each tile ([S&S](https://archive.org/details/SimCity_2000_Strategies_Secrets_SE)). Reverse engineers report that land value does not actually affect tax at all, even though the designers apparently meant it to ([dfloer simulation spec](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/dfloer/SC2k-docs/master/simulation%20spec.md)). The formula matches our own books closely. 11,500 people at an average rate of 6.33% predicts $939 a year, and we got $1,042. 2,530 people at 7% predicts $228, and we got $245. So **our ~$0.10 per person per year is exactly normal, not a bug.**

| Population | Tax at 7% | Tax at 9% |
|---|---|---|
| 10,000 | ~$900 | ~$1,160 |
| 50,000 | ~$4,500 | ~$5,800 |
| 100,000 | ~$9,000 | ~$11,600 |

*Calculated with the 1.29 rule ([Dargahi](https://archive.org/details/SimCity_2000_Power_Politics_and_Planning_Revised)). No published Budget-window screenshots at these sizes exist to check against.*

Two other facts shape cash flow. **Tax is collected monthly but only reaches the treasury in January** ([Dargahi Ch.4](https://archive.org/details/SimCity_2000_Power_Politics_and_Planning_Revised)). Also, **7% is the game's "average" rate**: the in-game help says so, and lower rates attract people ([in-game help](https://github.com/araxestroy/sc2kfix/blob/main/modules/sc2k_help.cpp)). The designers' table reads like this: **0-6% gives fast growth with less tax, 7-9% gives slow steady growth, and above 9% every zone declines**. The book calls rates above 9% "suicidal" ([Dargahi Ch.4](https://archive.org/details/SimCity_2000_Power_Politics_and_Planning_Revised)). One player measured the game's tax curve and found yearly income peaks near **9%** and "drops steeply" by 15% ([danbscott](https://danbscott.ghost.io/sim-citys-laffer-curve/)). On Medium, keep the industrial rate at or below 7% ([ludo.guide](https://www.ludo.guide/guide/simcity-2000/budget)).

Ordinances add a little money, and each one comes with a side effect. These are the designers' own estimates ([Dargahi Ch.9](https://archive.org/details/SimCity_2000_Power_Politics_and_Planning_Revised)). The **1% Income Tax** brings about $1 per 75 residents and is the biggest earner, but it moderately slows residential growth. **Legalized Gambling** brings about $1 per 38 commercial Sims but adds crime. The **1% Sales Tax** brings about $1 per 75 commercial Sims and slightly slows commerce. **Parking Fines** bring about $1 per 150 residents. At 100,000 people all four together add roughly $2,000 a year, about 20% on top of property tax. At 10,000 people they add only about $250, which is less than the growth they cost. Running services is cheap: a police or fire station costs $100 a year, a school $25, and roads $1 per 10 tiles ([Manual p.85-88](https://archive.org/details/SimCity.2000-Manual)). A 100,000 city's whole service bill comes to roughly $3,600 a year. **The big costs are building things and power plants**, not upkeep. The classic game has **no other income at all**: no deals with neighbouring cities and no income from ports ([Manual p.82-88](https://archive.org/details/SimCity.2000-Manual)).

## Demand has three ceilings, and each needs its own key

The designers explain demand plainly. "Residential demand is driven by the presence of commerce and industry", that is, by jobs. "Commercial demand is driven by the overall population size." Industrial demand comes from the national economy outside the city, which is "stable" on Medium ([PPP p.398](https://archive.org/details/SimCity_2000_Power_Politics_and_Planning_Revised); [Manual](https://archive.org/details/sc2000_manual)). Early on the game wants about **three factories for every shop**. As the city "nears 100,000, the ratio approaches 1:1" ([PPP p.398](https://archive.org/details/SimCity_2000_Power_Politics_and_Planning_Revised)). The tile rule of thumb is **residential tiles ≈ commercial + industrial tiles** ([Manual p.119](https://archive.org/details/sc2000_manual)). Fans suggest R:C:I of about 4:1:3 below 10,000 people and 4:2:2 from 10,000 to 60,000 ([somacon](https://www.somacon.com/p606.php)). On top of this sit three hard ceilings:

| Ceiling | What it holds back | What lifts it | Cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| Residential cap at **~15,000 residents** | New homes | **Stadium, zoo, marina** (location doesn't matter; "the more you build, the more population you can get") | Stadium $5,000 (one source says $3,000), zoo $3,000, marina $1,000 |
| Commerce cap | Shops | **Road link** to a neighbour, or an **airport** (worth it from ~20,000 people) | Road link $1,000; airport $250/tile |
| Industry cap | Factories | **Highway or rail link** off the map edge, or a **seaport** (needs a river or coast) | Highway $1,000-1,500 (sources differ), rail $1,500; seaport $150/tile |

*Sources: [PPP p.399](https://archive.org/details/SimCity_2000_Power_Politics_and_Planning_Revised); [Manual pp.101-110](https://archive.org/details/sc2000_manual); [Manual reference](https://archive.org/details/SimCity.2000-Manual); [S&S pp.167-168](https://archive.org/details/SimCity_2000_Strategies_Secrets_SE); [Dargahi Ch.2](https://archive.org/details/SimCity_2000_Power_Politics_and_Planning_Revised).*

The reverse-engineered game rules put rough numbers on the residential ceiling. Recreation demand starts at **10,000 people**. Each **stadium or zoo lifts the cap by about 16,000**, a **marina by 9,000**, a big park by 3,000, and other recreation tiles by about 1,000 each ([dfloer simulation spec](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/dfloer/SC2k-docs/master/simulation%20spec.md)). If those numbers hold, a 100,000 city needs something like **four or five stadiums and zoos plus marinas** in place before it gets there. The industry rule matters most for us. "**Industry demands a connection**" means build a **highway or rail** link, "not a road connection." A road link only helps commerce, and extra road links to the same neighbour off the same edge add nothing ([PPP ch.2](https://archive.org/details/SimCity_2000_Power_Politics_and_Planning_Revised)). The game flashes "Industry needs a seaport" or "Commerce demands a connection", but the message "can flash by quickly and be easily missed". The designers' advice: "if you notice your industry lagging … and unemployment rising, add more industrial carrying capacity" ([PPP p.399](https://archive.org/details/SimCity_2000_Power_Politics_and_Planning_Revised)). A negative bar for commerce or industry "may be caused by the lack of an airport or seaport and not have anything to do with" having too many zones ([PPP p.37 fn](https://archive.org/details/SimCity_2000_Power_Politics_and_Planning_Revised)). Unemployment should stay **below 7%** ([PPP](https://archive.org/details/SimCity_2000_Power_Politics_and_Planning_Revised)). Taxes push all of this up or down, though the demand bars take "at least a few months" to respond ([GOG forum](https://www.gog.com/forum/simcity_series/sim_city_2000_rci_demands)).

There is one open question about our map. A seaport and a marina both need water. The manual says a seaport must sit on a river or ocean, and "If you don't have a river or coastline, a seaport is useless" ([PPP p.399](https://archive.org/details/SimCity_2000_Power_Politics_and_Planning_Revised)). Our map has a river, so both may be possible. Whether the river is wide enough for them has to be tested in the game.

## Growth is fast when trips, power and water work, and stops dead when they don't

Three rules come before everything else: "no growth without power; no growth without transportation; growth is severely limited unless there is a water system" ([PPP p.398](https://archive.org/details/SimCity_2000_Power_Politics_and_Planning_Revised)). **Power** does not pass through empty zoned land, so power lines must run through undeveloped zones ([PPP p.407](https://archive.org/details/SimCity_2000_Power_Politics_and_Planning_Revised)). Built-up areas use about **1 MW for every 3 occupied tiles** ([Manual p.103](https://archive.org/details/sc2000_manual)). When the power runs out, the unpowered buildings decay. **Roads**: a building must be within **3 tiles of a road**, not counting diagonals, so the largest block that fully develops is **6x6** with road all around ([Manual p.105](https://archive.org/details/sc2000_manual)).

**Trips** cause the most quiet failures. Every building regularly sends a test trip, and it must reach **both of the other zone types** (homes to shops and factories, and so on) within **100 "steps"**. A car on a road uses 3 steps per tile. A bus uses 2. Highways, rail and subway use 1, and using a station costs 4 more. In practice that is **about 33 road tiles**, about 50 by bus, and about 96 by highway or rail. If the trip fails, "the building will stagnate or decay" ([PPP pp.400-401](https://archive.org/details/SimCity_2000_Power_Politics_and_Planning_Revised)). The designers say plainly: "One of the main things that keep cities from developing is that the different zones are too far away from each other and the simulated trips fail" ([PPP p.403](https://archive.org/details/SimCity_2000_Power_Politics_and_Planning_Revised)). There is a useful loophole: "the model assumes that all types of zones are accessible just beyond the edges of the city." Any zone near a road, highway or rail line that runs off the map edge gets successful trips to all types ([PPP p.403](https://archive.org/details/SimCity_2000_Power_Politics_and_Planning_Revised)). Mass transit can backfire: "A bad mass transit system is worse than none at all", because a building whose transit trip fails stops trying it ([Manual pp.107-108](https://archive.org/details/sc2000_manual)).

**Abandonment** happens when Sims find "something not to their liking": failed trips, power cuts, no water, crime, or negative demand. It is temporary if demand holds and the cause gets fixed. Sims rebuild on abandoned lots by themselves, but rubble left by a disaster must be bulldozed first ([Zen](https://ibiblio.org/GameBytes/issue18/misc/zen.html); [Manual p.121](https://archive.org/details/sc2000_manual)). No source gives a growth rate in tiles per month. Our own attempt 2 grew by about 10,000 people in one year (2007-2008), so raw speed is not the limit. With disasters on, a power plant collapses after **50 years** ([Manual p.103](https://archive.org/details/sc2000_manual)). A plant built in 2000 lasts until about 2050, so the risk lands right at the deadline.

## Bonds cost $400-600 a year each and only pay if they add 5,000+ people

Every bond is **$10,000**. The interest rate is the **prime rate + 1%, plus a premium that grows as your credit rating falls**. Interest builds up monthly and is paid each January. Bonds **never mature**, so they cost money every year until you repay them, and repayment means paying back the full $10,000, oldest bond first ([Manual p.83-85](https://archive.org/details/SimCity.2000-Manual)). Each new bond drops the rating a notch (AAA to AA to A, and so on) and raises the rate for the next one. At rating F no more bonds are allowed. The limit is one bond per $4M of city value, up to 50. With a negative balance you cannot build anything, and **at −$100,000 the game ends** ([Dargahi Ch.4](https://archive.org/details/SimCity_2000_Power_Politics_and_Planning_Revised)). The designers' advice: "If you have a small city… stay away from bonds — they will eat you alive." Bonds are useful only "for medium-to-large cities that have a positive cash flow" ([Dargahi Ch.4](https://archive.org/details/SimCity_2000_Power_Politics_and_Planning_Revised)). One designer also wrote, "To my chagrin, I found that you could actually use a bond to generate rapid growth that would offset the bond expense" ([S&S foreword](https://archive.org/details/SimCity_2000_Strategies_Secrets_SE)). Veteran players say to take no more than one bond at a time ([patcoston Q&A](https://patcoston.com/co/QA.aspx)), and that "loans rarely pay off" ([DOS Game Club](https://www.dosgameclub.com/forums/topic/my-experience-with-sc2k/)).

The test for whether a bond pays is simple arithmetic. A bond at 5% costs $500 a year. At about $0.09 per person per year, it only covers its own interest if it brings in **at least 5,500 extra people for good**. To also pay back the principal within about 12 years, it needs about 9,000. A bond passes this test only when the lack of cash is the one thing holding up a large growth step. Examples are a power plant when power is the limit, a stadium when residential demand has hit its cap, or a highway link when industry has hit its cap. Bonds used to pay for ordinary zoning fail the test.

## Players who reach 100,000 follow the ratio, unlock caps early and build dense

There is no published year-by-year timeline of a 100,000 city in SimCity 2000. The advice that keeps coming up is consistent, though. A recent summary of community testing says "Adhering to the game's desired RCI zone ratio is the primary driver of population growth". It says "recreation, ports, and neighbor connections matter most for large population gains", and that health and education "have minimal impact" on growth ([ludo.guide](https://www.ludo.guide/guide/simcity-2000/help)). Players add the **seaport around 10,000** people and the **airport around 15,000-20,000** ([patcoston objdef](https://patcoston.com/co/OBJDEF.aspx); [Manual](https://archive.org/details/sc2000_manual)). They build highways from about 50,000 ([patcoston advice](https://patcoston.com/co/advice.aspx)). They zone in small steps and let the city settle, because big zoning booms cause abandonment ([Zen](https://ibiblio.org/GameBytes/issue18/misc/zen.html)). For layout they use road-efficient stripes or **6x6 dense blocks**. A 6x6 block fits nine 2x2 or four 3x3 buildings with no wasted space ([somacon](https://www.somacon.com/p606.php)). They also turn light zones into dense ones to add tax base without new roads, pipes or power lines ([Zen](https://ibiblio.org/GameBytes/issue18/misc/zen.html)). For power, a coal plant gives 200 MW for $4,000 and nuclear gives 500 MW for $15,000 (from 1955, with meltdown risk when disasters are on). From 2020, microwave gives **1,600 MW for $28,000**, the cheapest power per MW before fusion arrives in 2050 ([patcoston Q&A](https://patcoston.com/co/QA.aspx)). Water pumps next to fresh water produce about twice as much as pumps on dry land ([Zen](https://ibiblio.org/GameBytes/issue18/misc/zen.html)), so the river is our cheapest water source. One player reports cities reaching 25,000 "without seeing any recessions" and says about 60,000 needs roughly two big power plants ([DOS Game Club](https://www.dosgameclub.com/forums/topic/my-experience-with-sc2k/)).

## What this means for reaching 100,000 by 2050

**Our ceiling at ~35,700 was the residential cap, and we never built the key to it.** In attempt 2, residential demand was still at +2,000 in 2009 at 30,650 people. By 2011 it had dropped to +172, and from 2012 it was negative while the population fell to about 29,000. Our population counts about 17-18 per tile on light homes, which can only hold about 10 residents each. So the displayed total seems to include more than residents, and residents were probably close to the ~15,000 mark where the game demands stadiums, zoos or marinas ([PPP p.399](https://archive.org/details/SimCity_2000_Power_Politics_and_Planning_Revised)). That last step is an inference and is not proven. We never built a single stadium, zoo or marina. Attempt 4 shows the same shape: residential demand fell from +2,000 to below zero between 2016 and 2017 at about 28,000 people. **Rule: build a stadium or zoo by ~10,000 people and add one about every 15,000 after that.** That means roughly five or six by 100,000. Add marinas on the river if the game allows them.

**"Industry at max while factories were abandoned" fits the industry cap and failed trips, not a lack of demand.** Industrial demand follows the outside economy, so the bar can sit at maximum while the city has no trade capacity for more factories. That capacity comes only from a **highway or rail link off the map edge** or a seaport. Our one road link helps commerce only ([PPP ch.2](https://archive.org/details/SimCity_2000_Power_Politics_and_Planning_Revised)). Factories more than about 33 road tiles from homes and shops also fail their trips and decay ([PPP pp.400-401](https://archive.org/details/SimCity_2000_Power_Politics_and_Planning_Revised)). **Rule: build a rail or highway link off the map edge early ($1,000-1,500), and test a seaport on the river once the city passes 10,000.** Commercial demand being negative most of the time is partly normal: the game wants about one shop for every three factories in a small city. But it also points to the commerce cap. **Rule: add a second road link on a different edge, then an airport once the city passes 20,000.**

**The density gap explains why 2,000 home tiles produced only 35,000 people.** Attempt 2 ended with **2,017 light home tiles and zero dense ones**, at 17-18 per tile. Attempt 7 was all dense and reached **30-37 per tile** on 130 tiles, which matches the fan figure for high-density 2x2 buildings. At 18 per tile, 100,000 needs about 5,600 home tiles. At 30 it needs about 3,300, and at 40 about 2,500. Even attempt 7's dense zones grew on land worth only about 2-3 out of 255. **Rule: zone only dense residential, in 6x6 blocks or three-deep bands with corner road access, pipe every block, and put trees and small parks inside each block.** Place the best homes along the river and keep industry and heavy traffic away from them. Track average land value in the save and aim well above our past peak of 27.

**Loans were the budget killer, and no loans at all left too little money to build.** Attempt 2 carried 7 loans by 2008. At $400-600 each that is roughly **$3,000-4,000 a year in interest**, against a predicted tax income of about $2,600 at 28,770 people. By 2016 it had 10 loans and by 2017 cash was negative. Attempt 4 followed the same path: 10 loans and negative cash by 2021. Attempt 7 took no loans, never held more than about $1,900, and reached only **3,850 people by 2010**. Our $10,000 start simply cannot pay for the city's early growth on its own. **Rule: take at most 1-2 bonds, each tied to a specific growth step it must unlock** (a power plant, a stadium, the rail link). Only take one if it can add about 5,500+ people, and never take one for routine zoning. Keep taxes at 7% (6% while a zone's demand needs help, never above 9%). Add the four income ordinances only after about 30,000 people. Repay the oldest bond as soon as cash allows.

**Put together, these numbers show a path to 100,000.** Attempt 2 already showed the game can add about 10,000 people a year when demand is open, so the 50-year window has plenty of slack. The plan needs about 3,300 dense home tiles and about the same number of job tiles. The job zones should shift from about 3:1 factories to shops early toward 1:1 near 100,000, all within about 30 road tiles of one another. Power should follow the 1 MW per 3 tiles rule. Microwave plants arrive in 2020 and are the cheapest power at that scale. The caps must be lifted before each one is hit, not after: a stadium or zoo every ~15,000 residents, a rail or highway link for industry, extra road links and an airport for commerce. On the money side, about $9,000 a year at 100,000 people is enough to run the city but cannot carry more than a couple of bonds. Several key numbers are still unconfirmed. These include people per building type, the exact size of each cap increase, the land-value levels needed for 2x2 and 3x3 buildings, and whether the river can hold a seaport or marina. Our own save files can measure all of them early in the next attempt, so the plan rests on our own numbers instead of 1990s fan pages.

## Conclusion

The main finding is that our seven attempts failed for one shared reason, and it was not that our play was too slow. In each one the city hit a game rule we had not planned for. The residential cap explains the decline after 35,700. The missing industrial trade link explains the factories that died while demand stood at maximum. The tax formula explains why loans could never be paid off. Light zoning on low-value land explains why our tiles held half the people they could. Each of these was fixable with a few thousand dollars spent at the right moment.

So the next attempt should be planned around milestones, not around simply growing as fast as possible. Each population threshold (10,000, 15,000, 20,000, then every 15,000) has a known unlock that must be built before the city reaches it. Every bond must be tied to one of those unlocks. And density, which comes from dense zoning, water, trees, parks and corner lots, is what lets 100,000 people fit on the land and the budget we have.


## Trips: why factory land stalled at ~20,000 (Oct 2026, after attempt 15)
The reverse-engineered growth code (sc2kfix, `hooks/hook_tilegrowthorplacement.cpp`; dfloer spec; PPP p.400)
shows an empty zone tile only develops after a **successful trip**: from a road within reach (3 tiles straight
or short diagonals) a random walk of 100 steps (3 per road tile, ~33 road tiles) must pass a destination zone
(empty zones count) or reach a map-edge connection. Only then does demand matter (build chance ~37% per pass
at a maximum bar). Failed trips mean no growth at any demand. Our `bot/trips.py` (Monte Carlo of that walk,
factories -> homes) on the stalled saves of attempts 11, 13, 14 and 15: home and shop land 65-100% trip
success; **the stuck factory land 22-33%**, all in factory-only areas (the west strip by the river x54-74
y64-75, south factory rows). The industry cap was not binding: ~7,400 factory workers vs 10,000 per crane/
rail link. **Rule: every factory cell must have homes within a few road tiles (mixed cells), never a
factory-only district; check new land with trips.py.** Land value only gates dense upgrades (1x1 needs 32,
2x2 needs 96, 3x3 needs 192). Tax per year = rate x population / 75.


## Land value, building size and money (Oct 2026, after attempt 18)
From the sc2kfix source, the DOS executable's population table, PPP and a regression on our own saves:
- **Building levels**: 1x1 (weight 1, ~19 displayed people), small 2x2 (8, ~140), large 2x2 (12, ~200), 3x3 tower
  (36, ~700). Dense home zones upgrade at land value **32** (small 2x2), **96** (large 2x2), **192** (tower); light
  zones never upgrade. Shops/factories use the same weights for jobs. Each upgrade roll succeeds with chance
  `3*(demand+2000)/(level+1)/32768`, **only after a successful trip**: with home demand around -1,300 (our 17-18
  plateau) almost nothing upgrades.
- **Land value** (monthly scan, per 2x2 tiles, 0-255): per tile in each 4x4 block **trees/small park +20, big park
  +40, water +12, hill +12, empty +4, pipe +4, rubble -20**; then lowered by pollution (~-0.57 per unit), crime
  (~-0.36) and distance from the city centre (~-0.37 per tile). Our homes sit at ~77-81; shoreline cells ~165; we had
  almost no trees or parks in housing blocks.
- **Recreation caps**: zoo/stadium +16,000 home cap each, marina +9,000, **big park +3,000** (cheap).
- **Money**: tax = rate x population / 75; land value does not affect tax. Running costs are small; **loan interest
  dominates**. Ordinances: sales/income tax +1% revenue for -1% demand; gambling +2% shop revenue.
- **Power** ($/tile powered): coal 5.7, oil 8.6, gas 11.4, nuclear 8.4, **microwave 4.9 (on our map from 2028)**;
  plants last 50 years.
- **Ranked changes**: 1) jobs first so home demand stays positive (upgrades need it); 2) waterfront tower districts
  near the centre with trees/parks in every block, no industry/plants nearby; 3) big parks, marinas, zoos for the
  caps; 4) no more loans than needed; 5) less pollution near homes (buses, microwave in 2028).
